The clock chimed half past two.
In the back office of McKechnie’s
Bookshop, Gordon – Gordon Comstock,
the last of the Comstock line,
twenty-nine years old and already
distinctly shabby – leaned over
the table, flicking the pack of
cheap "Player’s" cigarettes open
and shut with his thumb.
Disturbing the street's quiet,
the clock chimed again – from
the facade of the "Prince of Wales"
opposite. Gordon finally forced
himself to sit up straight and
shoved the pack deeper into his
inside pocket. He desperately
wanted a smoke. Alas, only four
cigarettes left, it was Wednesday,
and no money coming until Friday.
It would be too miserable to pine
for tobacco both tonight and all
day tomorrow. Already suffering
from tomorrow’s nicotine craving,
he got up and walked toward
the door – slight, petite, and
extremely nervous. The middle
button on his jacket was missing,
the right elbow was frayed,
his wrinkled trousers sagged
and were stained, and his soles,
without a doubt, were completely
worn through.
As he stood up, the change
jingled in his pocket. He knew
exactly how much was there –
fivepence halfpenny: two pennies,
a halfpenny, and a "lucky"
threepenny bit. Slowing his pace,
he pulled out that cursed Christmas
threepence. What an idiot!
How could he have let them palm
off that foolish coin on him?
Yesterday, when buying cigarettes.
"Don't mind a 'lucky' one, sir?"
the unpleasant shopgirl had
piped up. And of course, he
hadn't objected: "Oh no,
please do." Idiot, blockhead!
It feels wretched when you only
have five pence to your name,
and three of them you can't
even spend. How can you pay
with this trifle meant for
a pudding? It's not money,
it's an exposure. Like a fool,
you pull out a "lucky" coin
not from a handful of change.
You ask: "How much?" – and
they chirp: "Threepence." And,
rummaging through your pockets,
you fish out this miserable
absurdity on the tip of your
finger as if you were playing
buttons. The girl snickers –
instantly realizing you haven't
got another penny, and glances
sharply at the coin to see if
any bits of dough are still
stuck to it. And you, nose
in the air, sail out of the shop,
never daring to cross its
threshold again. No! The "lucky"
one didn't count. Twopence
halfpenny – two pence and
a halfpenny until Friday.
